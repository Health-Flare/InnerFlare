# Maya, 36: the time-poor sceptic

> "I don't need it to be clever. I need it to be finished in four seconds
> and not lie to me."

A fictional composite persona used for product and UX review. See
[README.md](README.md) for what these are and how to use them.

## Who she is

36. Two children, 7 and 4. Works four days a week. Came off hormonal
contraception two years ago and started tracking her cycle then.

She is a critical thinker in the practical sense: she reads the permission
list before installing, she notices when an app's marketing copy and its
behaviour disagree, and her first question about any feature is "what
happens when this goes wrong?" She is not hostile to technology; she's
hostile to technology that assumes it is the most important thing on her
phone.

## Why she tracks

1. **Logistics.** She wants to know when her period is coming so it doesn't
   ambush her on a work trip or a school run.
2. **Contraception, partially.** She and her partner use cycle awareness as
   *part* of their approach. This is the load-bearing motivation: it means
   prediction honesty is a safety property for her, not a nice-to-have.
   An app that shows a confident fertile window it can't support is doing
   something worse than being unhelpful.

## Her constraints: these shape every judgement she makes

- **She uses the app in 10–30 second windows.** One-handed. Often holding
  a child. Often at 11pm, half asleep. Often with the phone flat on a
  kitchen counter at arm's length.
- **Back-filling is her primary interaction mode.** She forgets for four to
  six days at a stretch and then catches up. Any flow that treats
  "logging today" as the main case and back-filling as an edge case is
  built for someone who isn't her.
- **Her phone is semi-public.** Her kids grab it. It sits face-up on the
  table at her mother-in-law's. What the app shows on a lock screen, in the
  app switcher, and to someone glancing over her shoulder is a real
  concern.
- **She is privacy-motivated for concrete reasons**: insurers, employers,
  and a flat refusal to have her reproductive data sitting on someone
  else's server. Not for abstract ones.
- **Every unlock is a tax.** She pays it several times a day. A security
  measure that costs her four seconds per use has to be earning that back.

## What she reviews

- The speed and friction of the core logging loop, measured in taps and
  seconds from cold launch.
- Back-filling and missed days: is catching up on five days genuinely as
  fast as logging one?
- Interruption and recovery: what survives when the app is killed
  mid-task, backgrounded, or interrupted by a child.
- The unlock and biometric experience as a recurring cost, and what the
  failure modes feel like.
- Shoulder-surfing and on-device discretion.
- What the app does when it is **wrong or broken** in her hands: error
  copy, recovery paths, whether she can tell if her data saved.
- Whether the app earns its place on a busy person's phone at all.
- Whether privacy claims are verifiable rather than asserted.

## What makes her delete the app

- Logging a day takes more than a handful of taps, or requires a field.
- She back-fills five days and it takes five times as long as one day.
- She sees a "Couldn't save" message and can't tell whether her data is
  there.
- The app re-prompts for biometrics when she didn't ask it to.
- A prediction turns out to have been fabricated from thin data and she
  finds out the hard way.
- The app's privacy copy turns out to be louder than its actual behaviour.

## Questions she asks in a review

- How many taps from cold launch to "today is logged"?
- If I'm interrupted halfway through, what's saved and what's lost?
- Show me this screen with zero data. Now with six days of gaps.
- What does the notification/app-switcher preview reveal?
- What permissions does the shipped app actually declare, and does every
  one of them correspond to something the app does?
- When it fails, does it tell me the truth about what happened?
